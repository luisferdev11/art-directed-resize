How it works, and how it would scale

What the engine does today and how each decision is taken, then the architecture I would build to run it for a whole agency: concurrency, cost, observability over the model, governance, and where it meets the designer.

Read this before anything else This page mixes two kinds of statement and labels every one of them. Today means it exists, it runs, and the figure comes from a generated manifest you can open. Proposed means it is an architecture decision I can defend, with not one line written. Nothing on this page is production software, and I would rather label it in every section than say it once at the top and hope you remember.

Part oneHow the engine works

Today One master goes in. Every format comes out in a single pass, each resolved on its own terms. The pipeline is six steps, and the interesting property is that only one of them involves a model.

1. Read the piece into a format-independent scene

A vector master carries its own tree, so the scene is measured: elements, positions, type sizes, hierarchy. Roles come from type size, area and relative hierarchy, and layer names are never read — rename everything to "Layer 1" and the result is identical. Depending on names would mean asking for a file authored for the tool, which is the complaint this whole thing exists to answer.

2. Choose the crop, with the subject as a hard constraint

A dense search over seven scales and thirteen by thirteen positions, scored on how much of the salient mass survives, the resolution left, and where the subject lands against the thirds. A floor and a ceiling on subject scale: too small and the person disappears, too large and it reads as a mistake. If no crop satisfies both, that failure is a signal, not an error — see step 5.

3. Place the text by search over the pixels

This is the part that separates the engine from a template. The placement is not chosen from a list of anchors. A cost field is built from the photograph — saliency, local luminance variance, luminance gradient — and the text block is searched densely over it with the detected face region ruled out entirely. The score balances that cost against keeping the headline large, against thirds asymmetry, and against staying faithful to the master's composition, so the layout does not wander when the photograph does not demand it.

4. Measure contrast line by line, and size the scrim by compositing

WCAG luminance by percentiles, per line rather than per block, because a three-line headline can have two lines over sky and the third over a red sign. Where a line falls short, the minimum scrim opacity is found by compositing in sRGB, which is where SVG actually composites — solving that blend in linear luminance under-sizes every scrim on dark ground, which is a bug this engine had and the validator caught.

5. Degrade in a stated ladder

When not everything fits, something gives way in a declared order: tighten leading, re-express hierarchy in weight when the legibility floor has compressed the size contrast, drop the legal, drop the support. And when no bleed crop can hold the subject at all, the photograph degrades to a side panel and the brand field is promoted — a structural decision emerging from a measured constraint, not a per-format template. Every rung that fires is recorded on the output.

6. Emit something a designer can actually work with

Layered SVG: one text element per line with absolute baselines, fonts alongside the artwork. Today Verified: it imports into Figma with its layers and live text intact.

And then it checks its own homework

A separate validator re-parses the delivered file and re-measures everything from it — overlap, safe area, type floor, uniform logo scale, image aspect, and per-line contrast against a backdrop it reconstructs from the file itself. It does not ask the solver what it thinks it wrote. That distinction is not academic: it is how the two most serious bugs in this codebase were found.

Part twoWhere the model is used, and where it is not

Today Model calls scale with masters, never with outputs. A campaign of 66 sizes makes one call to read the piece, and at most one more when the cheap path cannot answer. Never 66.

The model does one job: it reads a piece that carries no structure — a flat JPEG, no layers, no text nodes — and returns the semantic scene, assigning each block its role by communicative function. Headline, offer, operational detail, legal notice. That is a judgement about what the words are doing, and it is the one thing in this pipeline only a model can make.

Everything measurable is measured. The model proposes a region; a colour-distance mask over that region gives the pixel-accurate box. The model guesses a type size; solving the size from the measured advance width against real font metrics leaves nothing to guess. This is not caution about models. Each half is simply better at its half, and the measured half is free, deterministic and reproducible.

The second call, and the router that decides whether to make it

The crop treats one region as untouchable: whatever must survive every aspect ratio. Two Haar cascades from 2001 agreeing on a face answers that in milliseconds and for free, and on a straightforward portrait they do.

They also fail, and the failure is instructive. On a photograph of a person wearing a cap and sunglasses in front of a building, the two cascades together returned eight detections and not one was a face: a wristwatch, windows in the facade, poles on grass. The largest sat at 94% of the width, the crop is pinned to it as a hard constraint, and every format cut the subject in half.

Today A face now counts only when both cascades agree — an ensemble of two independently trained detectors, not a threshold fitted to the cases at hand. Measured over four photographs, the three with a real subject agree at IoU 0.79 to 0.93 and the failing one produces no agreement.

Agreement is not enough on its own either. On a group photograph, eleven corroborated faces span from 31% to 69% of the width. Taking the largest — which is what the code did — pins the crop to one person and cuts the rest of the team out of frame. Which face carries the composition, or whether the subject is the group itself, is a compositional judgement. Geometry does not have it.

So the cheap path answers only when it is unambiguous: exactly one agreed face. Zero, or several, is a declaration that this detector cannot say.

And then the question escalates to a model: not "where is the face" but "what must not be cropped, and what is it". On the cap-and-sunglasses photograph it answered person, man in a shirt, confidence 0.95, with a box on the actual subject, in nine seconds. On the group it answered group, a team posing together, with a box holding all eleven. Every format was usable again, and in 9:16 the photograph honestly degrades to a panel, because a row of eleven people does not survive a vertical bleed crop.

The schema was too narrow, and the model said so

On the group it first answered subject: "group", which was not in the enumeration. Validation rejected it and the chain spent fourteen seconds falling through to the next provider. The answer was right and the list was short. A schema that is too narrow does not protect you: it filters out what its author failed to anticipate, and the trace is what made that visible.

Why this is the interesting half

A cascade can only ever answer one question: is there a frontal human face. A campaign is not always a person. It is a product, a plate of food, an illustration, a piece that is nothing but typography on a colour. For those there is no geometry to consult and the question is semantic, which is exactly the boundary this system draws everywhere else: the cheap reliable path first, the model where only a model can answer. The cost argument survives because the escalation is per master and conditional, not per output and unconditional.

DecisionTaken byWhy
Role of each text blockthe modelcommunicative function is not recoverable from geometry
What must not be croppedtwo cascades if they agree, else the modelfree when geometry suffices; semantic when it does not
Where the ink sits, how largepixels + font metricsexact, free, and identical next Tuesday
Crop, placement, contrast, degradationdeterministic searchthe output must be reproducible and explainable
Typographic weightthe model, unverifiedstated as the one field this system does not check

And the boundary is defended: if the model chain returns nothing valid, the failure is recorded and travels onto the report page. A schema that does not validate triggers the same fallback as a dead socket, because a provider that answers 200 with garbage has failed just as completely as one that answers 500.

What the model would also do, in the real product

Proposed Three more jobs, in the order I would build them, and every one of them keeps the same shape: the model produces a proposal, deterministic code turns it into geometry, and a person can see and override it.

  1. Brief to rollout plan. A brief or a spreadsheet becomes the list of formats, centres and copy. It attacks the complaint about maintaining a spreadsheet by hand, and it is the single highest-leverage addition because it removes work that happens on every campaign rather than once per template.
  2. Copy variation per centre. Same campaign, different opening dates, different store counts, different legal wording per jurisdiction. Deterministic fill-in from structured data first; the model only where the copy has to be rewritten rather than substituted.
  3. A review conversation. Not to generate layouts, but to explain the ones produced and accept corrections in words. More on that below.

What I would not put a model on: the layout decisions themselves. They have to be reproducible, cheap and explainable, and a search over measured pixels is all three.

Part threeScaling it

Today Measured on a laptop, single process, serial: 66 formats in 131 seconds, about 2.0 seconds per format. No GPU. The deterministic path is CPU and memory only.

Proposed Three properties of the engine decide the whole architecture, and they are properties it already has:

  • Every format is a pure function of the scene. The problem is embarrassingly parallel by construction, with no shared state between outputs.
  • It is deterministic. Same input, same output. Which means retries are free and safe, results are cacheable, and a re-run costs nothing to reason about.
  • Model cost scales with masters, not with outputs. One call per master. Doubling the number of sizes does not touch the model bill at all.

Concurrency

UnitShape
Jobone campaign. Accepted by an idempotent API keyed on a content hash of master plus spec, so a duplicate submit returns the existing job instead of doing the work twice.
Taskone output format. Fanned out from the job, executed by stateless workers that hold nothing between tasks and can be killed at any moment.
Two prioritiesinteractive, where a designer is watching a single piece re-render, and batch, where a rollout runs overnight. They must not share a queue: one large batch should never make one designer wait.
The model stepisolated. It is the only rate-limited, non-free, non-deterministic step, so it runs once per master, behind a cache keyed by the master's hash, with a token bucket per provider and a spend ceiling per tenant.
Backpressurequeue depth is the signal. Workers scale on it; the API sheds and queues rather than timing out; a tenant that floods the queue is throttled rather than allowed to starve the rest.

A rough capacity figure, and I want to be honest about how rough. At the measured 2.0 seconds per format, 8 worker cores handle roughly 14,472 formats an hour. That is an extrapolation from a laptop, not a benchmark: real numbers depend on source resolution, canvas sizes and how much of the ladder fires. What the figure is good for is the shape of the answer — a mid-size agency's entire monthly output is hours of a handful of cores, and this workload is cheap to run.

Storage and delivery

Proposed Artifacts to object storage, laid out exactly as the demo already lays them out on disk: one self-contained folder per centre, artwork and fonts and manifest together, so a package can be zipped and sent without anyone extracting it from a larger document. Every asset addressed by the hash of master plus format plus config, which makes the store deduplicating and a re-run a no-op.

Part fourObservability over the model

Today The substrate already exists and is the part people usually have to retrofit: every output carries the list of reasons that produced it — which crop and why, where the text landed and at what cost, which contrast was measured, which ladder rungs fired, which provider answered and which ones failed first. It is printed on the report a human opens, not buried in a log nobody reads.

Today And it is now wired to a tracing backend. Every model call opens a span — one per attempt, not one per call, because the attempt that fails is precisely the one worth counting. Provider, model, latency, whether the schema validated, and every failure that preceded the answer. Verified against a deliberately broken provider: the first attempt lands as an error at 0.9 seconds, the second as a success, and the trace is marked as having fallen back.

If the tracing SDK is missing, the keys are absent or the server is down, the pipeline runs identically and says so once. Observability that can take the critical path down is worse than none.

Proposed What that substrate then supports:

SignalWhat it catches
Fallback rate Today the primary provider degrading. Rising fallback is the earliest warning you get, and it is invisible unless you record the recovery.
Schema-invalid ratea provider silently changing behaviour behind a pinned name.
Agreement with the deterministic parsera free online quality proxy. On structured masters both paths run, and disagreement on role assignment is a signal without needing a single label.

Triage distributionthe share of outputs flagged for review, tracked over time. If it drifts up, quality moved before anyone complained.
Traceone id from campaign to piece to model call, so "why does this asset look like this" is answerable in one query.

The regression gate

Proposed The labelled set runs in CI on every prompt change, model change and engine change. Not to compare models — a small set cannot — but to catch catastrophic failure and regression against yesterday's self, which is exactly what a small fixed set is good for. Prompt changes go through review like code, because they are code.

Part fiveGovernance

Proposed Six commitments. They are not compliance decoration; each one exists because of a specific way this kind of system goes wrong.

Pinned versionsexact model identifiers, never a floating alias. A provider improving a model overnight is still a change to your output, and it should arrive through a deploy.
Prompts in filesversioned, reviewed, diffable. An unversioned prompt is an unversioned business rule.
A person approvesnothing publishes itself. The triage exists so the designer reviews what needs review rather than all of it, but the gate is a person.
Data handlingdeclare what leaves the building — here, the master image — to which provider, under what retention and training terms, and in which region. For an Australian client that last one is a real constraint, not a formality.
No generative imagerythe engine crops, composes and reconstructs small regions with classical inpainting. It does not synthesise brand imagery. That is a declared limit, and it keeps brand safety out of scope entirely.
Audit trailevery published asset traceable to its master, config, model version and the reasons that produced it. If a client asks why an asset says what it says, that question has an answer.

And the one that matters most operationally: when the model is unavailable, the system degrades to reduced capability rather than to nothing. Structured masters keep working on the deterministic path. Only unstructured input is blocked, and it says so.

Part sixWhere it meets the designer

The verified constraint that decides this: Figma's REST API is read-only for design content. Reading a file, exporting images and managing comments happen server-side; creating or modifying layers requires the Plugin API, which runs inside the editor. The same shape holds elsewhere — Canva's Connect API covers assets, template autofill and export while element manipulation lives in its Apps SDK. That is not a limitation to route around. It decides the topology.

Proposed So: a plugin for reading and writing, a web app for orchestration. The plugin would read the selected frame into a scene and write finished pieces back as native layers; the web app would hold the plan, the batch, the review and the history. That is the same division the incumbent tool arrived at, and it arrived there for the same reason.

The conversational surface, and what it is actually for

A chat is genuinely good at three things here, and bad at a fourth.

  • Stating the plan before executing it. "Sixty-six sizes, four centres, here is what will be built, approve when ready." This is the pattern the incumbent does well and it deserves saying so: it is good UX and it is auditable.
  • Explaining a decision on demand. The reasons already exist for every piece; a chat is the natural way to ask "why is the legal missing on the leaderboard" and get "no bleed crop held the subject at eight to one, so the photograph became a panel and the ladder dropped the legal to keep the offer above its legibility floor."
  • Accepting a correction in words. "Keep the legal on the leaderboard, drop the dates instead." That would be a change to the ladder for one piece: it re-renders in about two seconds and does not touch the other sixty-five.
  • What it should not be is the only way in. A designer adjusting one crop wants a handle, not a sentence. The chat is a control surface over an engine whose decisions are already explicit, not a replacement for direct manipulation.

The loop that makes it usable

  1. Point at a master. No template authoring, no setup.
  2. See the plan. Sizes, centres, what will be produced.
  3. Get the batch, sorted by what needs attention rather than by name. Most pieces need nothing; the ones that do say why.
  4. Fix one thing, in the plugin or in words. One piece re-renders.
  5. Ship the packages, one folder per centre.

Step three is the one that changes how the tool feels. A rough first pass that hands you sixty-six files is homework. A rough first pass that hands you sixty-six files and tells you which four to open is a tool.

What I would build first

Not the AI. The file architecture and one written acceptance metric: what percentage of a campaign's formats need under N minutes of a designer's time, agreed in week one and measured against a real campaign, so there is a number to argue with instead of an impression.

Then the plugin, because output that lands where the designer already works is worth more than output that is technically correct somewhere else. Then the brief-to-plan step, because it removes work that recurs on every campaign rather than once per template. The layout engine is the part that already exists and the part that is hardest to buy.
